## Instructor notes
### Common misconceptions
- Students often think a negative delta G means a reaction is fast.
- Students may think catalysts make unfavorable reactions favorable.
- Students frequently confuse standard chemical state with biochemical standard state.
- Students may memorize functional groups without connecting them to reactivity.
- Students often know that water is polar but cannot explain why dielectric constant matters.
- Students may treat buffers as systems that keep pH fixed rather than systems that resist change.
### What to emphasize
- Thermodynamics answers whether a reaction is favorable; kinetics answers how fast it proceeds.
- Catalysts lower activation energy but do not alter equilibrium or delta G.
- Functional groups are the language of biomolecular reactivity.
- Water is not just a background solvent; its polarity and hydrogen bonding shape biology.
- pKa is a reference point for protonation behavior.
- Buffer reasoning becomes much easier when students track protonated and deprotonated forms explicitly.
### Optional extension prompts
- Predict how a change in pH could alter the charge on a carboxyl group in a metabolite.
- Explain why ATP hydrolysis is often coupled to biosynthetic work in cells.
- Compare a thermodynamically favorable but slow reaction with a fast but tightly regulated enzyme-catalyzed reaction.
## Suggested answer key language
When grading, look for:
- Correct use of the terms favorable, unfavorable, fast, slow, equilibrium, catalyst, acid, base, and buffer.
- Clear distinction between thermodynamics and kinetics.
- Explicit reasoning for pH and pKa relationships.
- Connection of molecular properties to biological outcomes.

## Student goals
By the end of this worksheet, you should be able to:
- Apply the basic principles of thermodynamics, equilibrium, and kinetics to biological systems.
- Distinguish reaction favorability from reaction rate.
- Explain how catalysts alter reaction pathways without changing equilibrium.
- Identify several common functional groups used in biochemical molecules.
- Relate water polarity and hydrogen bonding to biological solubility and intermolecular interactions.
- Use pH, $K_a$, and $pK_a$ to reason about acid-base behavior.
- Explain how buffers, especially physiological buffers, resist changes in pH.

## Part 2A. Class period 1: thermodynamics, equilibrium, kinetics, catalysis
### Worked example 1: spontaneity versus speed
A reaction has ΔG = -25.7 kJ/mol, but it proceeds very slowly in the cell unless an enzyme is present.
Questions:
1. Is the reaction thermodynamically favorable?
2. Is the reaction necessarily fast?
3. What additional concept explains why the reaction may still be slow?
### Worked example 1 key
1. Yes. A negative ΔG indicates that the reaction is thermodynamically favorable in the forward direction.
2. No. ΔG does not tell you the reaction rate.
3. Kinetics and activation energy explain why a favorable reaction may still proceed slowly.
### Worked example 2: catalyst effect
An enzyme lowers the activation energy for a reaction.
Questions:
1. What happens to the reaction rate?
2. What happens to ΔG?
3. What happens to the equilibrium constant?
### Worked example 2 key
1. The reaction rate increases.
2. ΔG does not change because the catalyst does not alter the energies of reactants or products.
3. The equilibrium constant does not change.
### Practice problem 1: biochemical standard conditions
Indicate whether each statement is true for biochemical standard conditions.
- Temperature is 298 K.
- Pressure is 1 atm.
- Solutes are at 1 M.
- pH is 7.0.
- The hydrogen ion concentration is 1 M.
### Practice problem 2: favorability and equilibrium
A reaction has a large negative ΔG degree prime.
Questions:
1. What does this suggest about the equilibrium position under standard biochemical conditions?
2. Would you expect the equilibrium constant to be greater than 1 or less than 1?
3. Does this guarantee the reaction is fast in a cell?
### Practice problem 3: rate law reasoning
For a simple reaction, the rate is proportional to the concentration of reactant A.
Questions:
1. If the concentration of A is halved, what happens to the rate?
2. Why is this a kinetics question and not a thermodynamics question?
### Application problem: ATP hydrolysis and coupling
ATP hydrolysis has a large negative free-energy change and is commonly used to drive other reactions.
Questions:
1. Why can ATP hydrolysis help drive an otherwise unfavorable reaction?
2. What does coupling mean in biochemical terms?
3. Why is ATP useful in cells even though some ATP-dependent reactions still require enzymes?
## Part 2B. Class period 2: organic chemistry, water, acids, bases, and buffers
### Worked example 3: functional groups
A molecule contains a hydroxyl group and a carboxyl group.
Questions:
1. Which functional group can donate a proton more readily?
2. Which functional group is more directly associated with alcohol chemistry?
3. Why do functional groups matter in biochemistry?
### Worked example 3 key
1. The carboxyl group.
2. The hydroxyl group.
3. Functional groups determine reactivity, polarity, intermolecular interactions, and biological function.
### Worked example 4: water and solubility
Compare two molecules:
- Molecule A contains several hydroxyl groups.
- Molecule B is mostly hydrocarbon.
Questions:
1. Which molecule is more likely to dissolve in water?
2. Why?
3. What role does polarity play?
### Worked example 4 key
1. Molecule A.
2. It can interact more favorably with water through hydrogen bonding and polar interactions.
3. Polar molecules interact more strongly with water than nonpolar molecules do.
### Practice problem 4: identifying concepts
Match each concept to the best description.
- Hydrogen bonding.
- Dielectric constant.
- Polarity.
- Solubility.
Descriptions
- Ability of a solvent to weaken ionic interactions.
- Uneven distribution of charge in a molecule.
- Attraction involving partial charges and electronegative atoms.
- Ability of a substance to dissolve in a given solvent.
### Practice problem 5: acids, bases, $K_a$, and $pK_a$
A weak acid has a $pK_a$ of 4.8.
Questions:
1. Is this acid more protonated at pH 3.8 or pH 5.8?
2. At which pH is the deprotonated form more abundant?
3. What does a lower $pK_a$ generally indicate about acid strength?
### Practice problem 6: buffer reasoning
A buffer contains equal amounts of HA and $A^-$.
Questions:
1. What is the relationship between pH and $pK_a$?
2. What happens to the ratio if strong acid is added?
3. What happens if strong base is added?
### Application case 2: blood buffering
A patient presents with rapid breathing and dizziness after a panic episode. Another patient presents with prolonged vomiting. Consider blood buffering in each case.
Questions:
1. Why is blood pH tightly regulated?
2. How does a physiological buffer help resist abrupt pH change?
3. Which case is more likely to shift blood chemistry through altered carbon dioxide handling?
4. Which case is more likely to alter acid-base balance through loss of stomach acid?
